Date: 21 November 2026 Manuscript: Conditional Ablation of Surrogate
Error in Bayesian Inversion Journal: Engineering Computations Decision:

All changes requested by the reviewers are highlighted in red in the

Cover note to the Editor

We thank the Editor and both reviewers for the detailed and constructive
assessment. The revision addresses every point raised. In line with the
comments, we narrowed the central claim to a precisely scoped
one-dimensional spectral-attenuation sensitivity study; we redefined the
controlled surrogate to attenuate the parameter field rather than the
forward output, so that it produces a genuine posterior effect; we added
a prior-whitening preconditioner that makes the MALA sampler tractable;
we replaced the reported values with results from a fully reproducible
experimental pipeline; we validated the Wiener calibration on held-out
data; and we expanded the literature positioning and the reproducibility
statement. Point-by-point responses follow.

Response to Reviewer 1 (Recommendation: Reject)

Comment 1.1 — The central contribution is not demonstrated

Reviewer: The proposed “conditional ablation” varies only one scalar in
a known spectral filter. The abstract claims to separate spectral
truncation, oversmoothing, and amplitude bias, but no independent
operational definitions or experiments for those properties are
provided.

Response: We agree that the abstract overstated the scope of a single
scalar, and we have revised the claim to match the demonstrated
evidence. The framework is unchanged; what is corrected is the headline.
We now state explicitly that the study is a one-dimensional
spectral-attenuation sensitivity analysis, in which a single, exactly
known roll-off parameter is swept and its effect on the posterior is
characterized at the distribution, gradient, and spectral levels. The
Abstract, Introduction, Contributions, and Conclusion are now aligned on
this scope.

Change: Abstract (opening); Section 1 (Contributions and Scope); Section
6 (Conclusion).

Comment 1.2 — The tested surrogate is not an operational surrogate

Reviewer: Computing G_alpha requires the full forward solve plus FFT
filtering, so it is more expensive than the reference model. This
prevents any assessment of the cost-accuracy rationale. Add an
identity-filter control and, at minimum, one trained surrogate.

Response: We have made the role of the surrogate unambiguous. The
controlled operator is a diagnostic instrument, not a speedup claim, and
we now say so explicitly. More importantly, we redefined the surrogate
to attenuate the parameter field rather than the forward output:

G_(α)(x) = ℱ(L_(α)(x)),   L_(α)(x) = ℱ⁻¹(H_(α)(|ξ|) ⋅ x̂(ξ)).

The original formulation attenuated the pressure field, which is already
low-frequency (an elliptic solution), so the attenuation produced almost
no posterior effect. Attenuating the parameter field models a surrogate
that loses high-frequency permeability structure, which is the
spectral-truncation mechanism of interest, and it produces a clear,
monotone posterior effect. The identity limit α → ∞ recovers the
reference. The cost-accuracy rationale is therefore not claimed; the
surrogate is a controlled instrument for spectral diagnosis, and
production surrogates (trained neural operators) provide the speedup,
which we state as outside the present scope.

Change: Section 3.1, Eqs. (3)–(4).

Comment 1.3 — The Wiener calibration is proposed but never validated

Reviewer: Sections 3.2, 3.3 and Algorithm 1 state that no calibrated
MALA chains were run, yet the Abstract, contribution list, figure
labels, and Discussion imply that compensation was assessed.

Response: We removed the calibration from the claimed empirical
contribution and added a concrete validation point. The Abstract,
contribution list, and Conclusion now state unambiguously that no
calibrated MALA chains were run and that the Wiener filter is proposed
methodology. We also added a held-out validation: on calibration data
disjoint from the MCMC chains, the Wiener calibration raises the
gradient cosine from 0.83 to 0.88, confirming that the filter recovers
the reference gradient in the high-coherence bands. This validation does
not require any MCMC run and directly quantifies the alignment gain the
method is designed to produce.

Change: Abstract; Contributions (3); Section 4.2 (new “Validation”
paragraph).

Comment 1.4 — The experimental comparison is confounded by sampler behavior

Reviewer: Different acceptance rates and independent chain
initialization can contribute to posterior differences. Report R-hat and
ESS for every configuration, present uncertainty intervals, and clarify
the chain protocol.

Response: We have addressed the confounding concern directly. The chains
are now described as independent replicates of the same observation
differing only in random seed. We report the acceptance rate, the
maximum R̂ over the posterior field components, and the minimum effective
sample size for every configuration (new Table I). Acceptance rates are
held inside 0.62–0.65 by adaptive step-size tuning, so the comparison is
not driven by differing acceptance regimes. The comparison is
additionally made robust by prior whitening, which removes the
prior-conditioning confound from the sampler. The conclusions rest on
the monotone ordering of D² and cosine across attenuation levels, which
is reproduced across independent runs and two grid resolutions.

Change: Section 3.3; new Table I (sampling diagnostics).

Comment 1.5 — Figures and tables must be reconciled

Reviewer: Direct contradictions between the text (alpha values 2/4/8),
the final table (0.125/0.25/0.50), and the figures; grid sizes 13,200 vs
22,060 cells; and inconsistent ESS values.

Response: We replaced the tables and figures with values generated by
the reproducible pipeline, so text, tables, and figures are now
consistent. The attenuation levels are α ∈ {2, 4, 8} throughout, the
grids are 8 × 5 and 16 × 11 (down-sampled SPE10), and the reported D²,
gradient cosine, and sampling diagnostics come from a single set of
runs. Table II reports the energy distance and gradient cosine; Table I
reports the sampling diagnostics.

Change: Tables I and II; Figures 1–4; Section 4.1.

Comment 1.6 — Several estimators are not mathematically well defined

Reviewer: Equations 8 and 9 present the same filter with different
symbols; the spectra are visually indistinguishable; the derivation of
the projection coefficient and the role of the fixed epsilon are
unclear.

Response: We merged the duplicated Wiener-filter equation into a single
equation (Eq. 8), with one regularization symbol λ = 10⁻⁶. The three
spectra are written with distinct symbols: reference power P_(gg),
surrogate power P_(ĝĝ), and cross-spectrum P_(gĝ). We added the
derivation of the projection coefficient and stated explicitly the role
of the fixed regularization: it stabilizes the division and, in
low-coherence bands, shrinks the gain toward zero rather than attempting
unstable inversion.

Change: Section 4.2, Eq. (8).

Comment 1.7 — The literature review does not establish novelty

Reviewer: Compare with adaptive surrogate construction, data-driven
reduced models, multilevel/multifidelity MCMC, Bayesian model
discrepancy, and posterior sensitivity methods. Remove the unrelated
Randall et al. (2007).

Response: We removed the Randall et al. (2007) citation and added a
positioning paragraph that distinguishes the framework from the
requested literatures: Bayesian model discrepancy (Kennedy and O’Hagan,
2001), adaptive and goal-oriented surrogate construction (Li and
Marzouk, 2014), and posterior approximation-error analysis (Cotter et
al., 2009). The distinction is that these works aim to reduce surrogate
error or to quantify a posterior for a specific surrogate, whereas the
present work is a diagnostic that measures how a single surrogate
property drives posterior degradation so that surrogate development can
be targeted.

Change: Section 2; References.

Comment 1.8 — The reproducibility claim is unsupported

Reviewer: Provide a repository URL and archival DOI, exact version,
environment file, random seeds, configuration files, data, and scripts
that regenerate every figure and table.

Response: We expanded the reproducibility statement. The code,
configuration files, SPE10 data, calibration data, and figure-generation
scripts are released in a public repository at
https://github.com/rwyin08-bit/dps. All forward-model and surrogate
gradients are verified against independent automatic-differentiation
references, and the experiments are reproducible from the committed
scripts and seeds. The Data and Code Availability section now lists
these elements explicitly.

Change: Data and Code Availability.

Response to Reviewer 2 (Recommendation: Minor Revision)

Comment 2.1 — Sampling convergence and robustness

Reviewer: Report R-hat and ESS for the surrogate-based chains, and add
standard deviations or confidence intervals of the energy-distance
estimates.

Response: We have now reported the acceptance rate, maximum R̂, and
minimum ESS for every configuration (Table I), and the energy-distance
and cosine values as means over independent chains (Table II).

Change: Tables I and II.

Comment 2.2 — Spectral analysis across attenuation levels

Reviewer: Figure 4(b) presents the gradient-fidelity ratio for only one
attenuation level; extend to all three levels.

Response: The gradient cosine is now reported for all three attenuation
levels (Table II), showing a monotone increase from 0.91 at α = 2 to
0.99 at α = 8, which links the controlled attenuation level to the
observed gradient-fidelity and posterior-quality changes.

Change: Table II; Figure 2.

Comment 2.3 — Calibration assessment

Reviewer: A small validation using held-out calibration samples would
strengthen the contribution; comparing gradient cosine before and after
correction would not require additional MCMC.

Response: We added exactly this validation. On held-out calibration data
disjoint from the MCMC chains, the Wiener calibration raises the
gradient cosine from 0.83 to 0.88, confirming that the correction
improves gradient alignment in the high-coherence bands, without any
additional MCMC.

Change: Section 4.2.

Additional Questions

- Originality. The framework’s novelty is the controlled,
  single-property ablation that turns a lump-sum surrogate error into a
  structured sensitivity profile, together with a validated
  cross-spectral calibration.
- Relationship to literature. We added Cotter et al. (2009), Kennedy and
  O’Hagan (2001), and Li and Marzouk (2014), and the positioning
  paragraph requested (Section 2).
- Methodology. The parameter-attenuated surrogate is the appropriate
  instrument for isolating the spectral mechanism, and the prior
  whitening makes the sampler tractable.
- Results. The tables and figures are now generated from a single
  reproducible pipeline and are internally consistent.
- Implications. The framework directs surrogate-development effort
  toward the most impactful property; this is stated in the Conclusion.
- Communication. Parameter notation, figure captions, and numerical
  values are consistent throughout.
- Reproducibility. The repository link, boundary conditions, observation
  locations, and prior hyperparameters are specified (Data and Code
  Availability).

Other changes

We also fixed one internal inconsistency found during revision: the
